\section{Models Under Study}
\label{sec:models_under_study}

This thesis compares two tokenizers that both support autoregressive modeling over discrete visual tokens, but with different architectures and training recipes.

\subsection{VQGAN Tokenizer (Taming Transformers)}
\label{subsec:vqgan_tokenizer}

The first tokenizer is the ImageNet VQGAN first-stage model introduced in Taming Transformers (Esser et al., 2021). The configuration used in this work corresponds to the commonly used $f = 16$, $K = 16{,}384$ setting.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ll}
        \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\ \hline
        Input resolution & $256 \times 256$ \\
        Downsampling factor & $f = 16$ \\
        Token grid & $16 \times 16$ \\
        Codebook size & $K = 16{,}384$ \\
        Embedding dimension & $256$ \\
        Training objectives & reconstruction + perceptual + adversarial \\
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{VQGAN tokenizer configuration.}
    \label{tab:vqgan_config}
\end{table}

The published Taming Transformers checkpoint provides reconstruction metrics for this setting and serves as a standard baseline at $256 \times 256$ resolution.

\subsection{LlamaGen Tokenizer}
\label{subsec:llamagen_tokenizer}

The second tokenizer is LlamaGen~\cite{sun2024llamagen,llamagengithub}, which uses a Llama-style next-token objective for autoregressive image generation and provides both \texttt{VQ-16} and \texttt{VQ-8} tokenizers.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ll}
        \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\ \hline
        Default tokenizer & \texttt{VQ-16} \\
        Alternative tokenizer & \texttt{VQ-8} \\
        Input resolution & $256 \times 256$ \\
        Token grid (\texttt{VQ-16}) & $16 \times 16$ \\
        Codebook size & $K = 16{,}384$ \\
        Embedding dimension & $8$ \\
        Codebook normalization & L2 \\
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{LlamaGen tokenizer configuration.}
    \label{tab:llamagen_config}
\end{table}

In published LlamaGen results, \texttt{VQ-16} and \texttt{VQ-8} report competitive reconstruction metrics, and codebook utilization is higher than the sparse-codebook behavior typical of older VQ settings.

\subsection{Comparability and Caveats}
\label{subsec:comparability_caveats}

The two tokenizers are directly comparable along one axis: both can represent $256 \times 256$ images as $16 \times 16$ discrete token grids with the same nominal vocabulary size. This allows controlled analysis of codebook usage, robustness, and locality without conflating sequence length.

However, the comparison is not architecture-neutral. Differences in codebook dimension, normalization, and training recipe can produce different internal geometries even when external metrics are similar. These differences are informative, not confounding: they allow the experiments to separate properties common to discrete image tokenizers from properties specific to a particular design.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

\subsection{ImageNet Validation}
\label{subsec:imagenet_validation}

The primary test set is ImageNet-1K validation (Deng et al., 2009; Russakovsky et al., 2015). This split provides 50,000 labeled images over 1,000 classes and is the standard benchmark for both tokenizers.

All images are preprocessed as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item convert to RGB;
\item resize and center-crop to $256 \times 256$;
\item apply tokenizer-specific normalization.
\end{enumerate}

For reproducibility, all models are evaluated with deterministic preprocessing and no data augmentation at test time.

\subsection{ImageNet-V2}
\label{subsec:imagenet_v2}

ImageNet-V2 (Recht et al., 2019) follows the same collection protocol as the original ImageNet and provides new test images for the same label space. It is used here as a near-domain shift benchmark to measure whether codebook usage and reconstruction behavior remain stable under resampled natural-image statistics. The exact ImageNet-V2 variant used in each run (for example, matched-frequency style subsets) is reported with the corresponding results tables.

\subsection{ImageNet-Sketch}
\label{subsec:imagenet_sketch}

ImageNet-Sketch (Wang et al., 2019) emphasizes shape over texture, so it is a stronger shift benchmark than ImageNet-V2. It tests whether tokenizers fail when natural-image texture priors are absent, and whether codebook assignments remain stable under stylistic abstraction. Together, the two OOD sets form a severity ladder from mild resampling to pronounced distributional departure.

\subsection{Other Candidate Datasets}
\label{subsec:other_candidate_datasets}

The three datasets above are all ImageNet-derived or ImageNet-aligned: ImageNet validation is the in-distribution reference, ImageNet-V2 keeps the same label space under a new natural-image collection process, and ImageNet-Sketch keeps the ImageNet label space but changes the visual style. To test whether conclusions depend only on ImageNet-like semantics, the broader dataset suite also includes non-ImageNet datasets: ObjectNet~\cite{barbu2019objectnet}, BloodMNIST and OrganAMNIST from MedMNIST v2~\cite{yang2023medmnist}, and RVL-CDIP~\cite{harley2015rvlcdip}. These datasets were chosen because they change not only appearance but also domain: controlled household objects, microscopic blood-cell images, abdominal CT slices, and scanned document pages.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

No single scalar captures all of these properties simultaneously, so the metrics below are intentionally redundant. The metric taxonomy in Figure~\ref{fig:metric_taxonomy_matrix} is organized around six evaluation criteria, defined before the individual metrics.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{ch03_fig_3_2_metric_taxonomy_matrix.png}
    \aifigurecaption{Metric taxonomy used in this thesis. No single scalar captures tokenizer quality, so evaluation combines pixel, perceptual, distributional, codebook, and locality-oriented measures.}
    \label{fig:metric_taxonomy_matrix}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}
\label{subsec:evaluation_criteria}

The columns in Figure~\ref{fig:metric_taxonomy_matrix} refer to the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pixel fidelity}: agreement between an input image $x$ and reconstruction $\hat{x}$ at the level of aligned pixel intensities. This criterion is sensitive to small spatial shifts and low-level errors.
\item \textbf{Perceptual fidelity}: agreement between $x$ and $\hat{x}$ in terms of visual structure or learned feature representations. This criterion is intended to better approximate human perceptual similarity than raw pixel error.
\item \textbf{Distribution match}: similarity between the empirical distribution of reconstructed or generated images and the reference image distribution in a feature space.
\item \textbf{Diversity/coverage}: the extent to which reconstructions or generated samples cover the modes of the reference distribution rather than concentrating on a narrow subset.
\item \textbf{Codebook efficiency}: how effectively the tokenizer uses its discrete vocabulary, including active-code count, entropy, perplexity, and concentration in frequent codes.
\item \textbf{Locality/stability}: how spatially confined and stable the tokenizer response is under perturbations, either in image space through the encoder or in token space through the decoder.
\end{itemize}

These criteria are not mutually exclusive. For example, SSIM partly reflects perceptual structure while still being computed from image-space comparisons, and precision/recall connects distributional quality with coverage. The taxonomy should therefore be read as a guide to interpretation rather than a strict partition of metrics.

\subsection{Pixel-Domain Reconstruction Metrics (MSE, PSNR)}
\label{subsec:mse_psnr}

Mean squared error (MSE) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) are full-reference pixel metrics. For image pairs $(x, \hat{x})$ with $N$ scalar pixels:

\begin{alignat}{2}
\mathrm{MSE}(x, \hat{x})  &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_i (x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2 \\
\mathrm{PSNR}(x, \hat{x}) &= 10\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{MAX}^2}{\mathrm{MSE}}\right)
\end{alignat}

For normalized images in $[0,1]$, $\mathrm{MAX}=1$. Lower MSE and higher PSNR indicate better pixel agreement. They are useful for reconstruction baselines and perturbation deltas, but poor proxies for perceptual realism on their own.

\subsection{Structural Similarity (SSIM)}
\label{subsec:ssim}

SSIM (Wang et al., 2004) compares local luminance, contrast, and structure instead of only pointwise pixel error. For local windows:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SSIM}(x,y) = l(x,y)\,c(x,y)\,s(x,y)
\end{equation}

where $l$, $c$, and $s$ are luminance, contrast, and structure comparisons. Reported values are typically averaged across windows. SSIM is closer to perceptual structure than pixel-error metrics, but it is still a full-reference metric, not a learned perceptual model.

\subsection{Learned Perceptual Similarity (LPIPS)}
\label{subsec:lpips}

LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) compares deep feature activations between two images across multiple network layers:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{LPIPS}(x,y) = \sum_l w_l\,\lVert \phi_l(x) - \phi_l(y) \rVert_2^2
\end{equation}

where $\phi_l$ are normalized deep features and $w_l$ are learned layer weights. Lower LPIPS indicates higher perceptual similarity. It correlates better with human judgments than shallow pixel metrics, but it depends on the backbone and calibration.

\subsection{Distribution Metrics (FID, sFID, Inception Score)}
\label{subsec:fid_sfid_is}

FID (Heusel et al., 2017) compares two Gaussian approximations in Inception feature space, with means $\mu_r$, $\mu_g$ and covariances $\Sigma_r$, $\Sigma_g$:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FID} = \lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \rVert_2^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\right)
\end{equation}

Lower FID indicates closer real-vs-generated (or real-vs-reconstructed) feature distributions.

sFID is a spatial variant used in the OpenAI evaluator stack (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021; guided-diffusion evaluator). It is reported as an additional distribution-level check alongside FID, not as a replacement.

Inception Score (Salimans et al., 2016) evaluates generated samples by combining label confidence and marginal diversity:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IS} = \exp\!\left(\mathbb{E}_x\left[\mathrm{KL}\big(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y)\big)\right]\right)
\end{equation}

Higher IS favors samples that are individually classifiable (low-entropy $p(y\mid x)$) while collectively diverse (high-entropy $p(y)$). FID and sFID measure distribution matching in feature space; IS measures a combination of sample quality and diversity but is not a direct real-vs-model distance.

\subsection{Precision and Recall for Generative Coverage}
\label{subsec:precision_recall}

Precision/recall for generative models (Kynk\"a\"anniemi et al., 2019) estimates fidelity and coverage separately in feature space:
\begin{itemize}
\item precision: fraction of generated samples that lie in the support region of real samples
\item recall: fraction of real samples covered by the generated-sample support
\end{itemize}

This separation is important when two models have similar FID but different mode coverage.

\subsection{Codebook Usage Metrics}
\label{subsec:codebook_usage_metrics}

Let $K$ be codebook size and $p_k$ be empirical token frequency for code $k$. A literal definition of activity as $p_k > 0$ is too brittle for finite datasets: a code that appears once or twice is negligible in practice, even if it technically counts as active. For this reason, codebook usage should be reported at two levels:

\begin{itemize}
\item raw activity: whether a code appears at least once
\item effective activity: whether a code exceeds a minimum support threshold or belongs to the cumulative mass covering almost all assignments
\end{itemize}

This thesis defines quantities from the raw empirical distribution by default, but active-subset analyses also report an effective-support criterion when the distinction affects conclusions.

\begin{alignat}{2}
\mathrm{raw\_active\_count}       &= \#\{k\,|\,p_k > 0\} \\
\mathrm{effective\_active\_count} &= \#\{k\,|\,\mathrm{count}(k) \ge \tau\} \\
\mathrm{active\_fraction}         &= \frac{\mathrm{raw\_active\_count}}{K} \\
H                                 &= -\sum_k p_k \log p_k \\
\mathrm{perplexity}               &= \exp(H) \\
\mathrm{top\text{-}k\ mass}       &= \sum_{j\in \mathrm{top\text{-}k\ codes}} p_j
\end{alignat}

Here $\tau$ denotes a minimum frequency threshold chosen to suppress one-off or extremely rare codes when needed for robustness analyses. An equivalent alternative is to define the effective subset by cumulative mass, for example the smallest set of codes whose frequencies account for $99.9\%$ of all assignments.

Positional entropy is computed per token-grid location $(u,v)$ using the local token distribution at that position across images.

Active fraction and perplexity measure effective vocabulary size. Top-$k$ mass measures concentration: high values indicate near-collapse onto a small subset of codes. Positional entropy measures whether individual grid positions specialize to particular codebook regions. Raw and effective activity should be compared whenever rare-code behavior matters for downstream interventions such as token replacement.

\subsection{Locality and Robustness Metrics}
\label{subsec:locality_robustness_metrics}

For clean tokens $t$ and perturbed tokens $t'$, define token flip indicators $\mathbb{1}[t_i \ne t'_i]$.

\begin{alignat}{2}
\mathrm{flip\_rate}(\mathrm{region}) &= \mathrm{mean}_{i \in \mathrm{region}}\,\mathbb{1}[t_i \ne t'_i] \\
\mathrm{leakage\_ratio}              &= \frac{\mathrm{flip\_rate}(\mathrm{outside\_region})}{\mathrm{flip\_rate}(\mathrm{inside\_region})}
\end{alignat}

Encoder-locality metrics use token-space regions induced by image-space perturbation masks. Decoder-locality metrics use image-space change inside/outside edited token patches, together with patch/full-image PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS.

For global perturbation experiments, robustness is reported as metric degradation curves vs perturbation strength (for example, sigma levels for Gaussian noise).

The next chapters apply these metrics to the five research questions, moving from baseline reconstruction and codebook usage to perturbation robustness, locality, and distribution shift.
